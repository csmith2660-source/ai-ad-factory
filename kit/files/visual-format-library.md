# The Ad Factory: Visual Format Library

This library defines the 28 core visual formats used in the Ad Factory workflow. Use this as a reference guide when reviewing the Creative Matrix Manus generates for you.

## 1. The "Before/After" Split Panel

**What it is:** A literal split-screen image. The top or left side shows the frustrating "Before" state (the problem). The bottom or right side shows the effortless "After" state (your product).
**Best for:** Problem-aware audiences. Products with highly visual transformations (skincare, cleaning, organization, fitness).
**Example:** Top: A chaotic, messy desk covered in sticky notes. Bottom: A clean, minimalist desk with a single digital planner app open on a monitor.

## 2. The "Us vs. Them" Comparison

**What it is:** A side-by-side comparison, often using a table or checklist, positioning your specific mechanism against the flawed industry standard.
**Best for:** Solution-aware audiences. Crowded markets where you need to differentiate.
**Example:** Left column ("Generic Supplements"): Artificial fillers, stomach upset, 10 pills a day. Right column ("Our Brand"): Whole food sourced, gentle on stomach, 1 scoop a day.

## 3. The "Bold Claim" Text-Dominant

**What it is:** A visually simple ad where heavy typography dominates the frame. The image is secondary or just a textured background. It relies entirely on a single, undeniable argument.
**Best for:** Unaware or Problem-aware audiences. Complex B2B services, software, or highly contrarian angles.
**Example:** "You're fully booked. So why aren't you making money?" in large, bold text on a dark charcoal background.

## 4. The "Feature Callout" / Diagram

**What it is:** A clean hero image of the product with lines pointing to specific, unique mechanisms or ingredients.
**Best for:** Physical products with unique engineering, apparel, tech hardware, or supplements.
**Example:** A running shoe cut in half, with lines pointing to the "carbon fiber plate" and "responsive foam core."

## 5. The "Testimonial Overlay"

**What it is:** A real, verbatim customer quote dominating the frame. The visual is often a portrait of the customer or a lifestyle shot of the product in use.
**Best for:** Solution-aware or Most-aware audiences who need proof before buying.
**Example:** "We hit a 25% gross margin in 6 months." — John Smith, Builder. (Overlaid on a photo of John on a job site).

## 6. The "UGC Lifestyle"

**What it is:** An authentic, slightly unpolished photo that looks like it was taken by a user on an iPhone, overlaid with a native-looking text box (like an Instagram Story text sticker).
**Best for:** DTC ecommerce, apparel, beauty, lifestyle brands.
**Example:** A selfie in a mirror wearing the brand's jacket, with an IG-style text box reading: "Finally found a winter coat that doesn't make me look like a marshmallow."

## 7. The "Stat Block"

**What it is:** Pure data and numbers proving the outcome. No lifestyle photography, just clean, authoritative design.
**Best for:** B2B, SaaS, financial products, or any brand with undeniable results data.
**Example:** Three large numbers stacked: "+31% Gross Profit. 8 Hours Saved Weekly. 6 Months Booked Out."

## 8. The "Handwritten Note"

**What it is:** An intimate, personal message that appears to be handwritten on paper or a digital notepad. Often used for founder stories or personal guarantees.
**Best for:** Founder-led brands, high-ticket coaching, or highly emotional purchases.
**Example:** A scanned handwritten letter starting "I built this because I was tired of..."

## 9. The "Text Message Conversation"

**What it is:** A screenshot of a relatable iMessage exchange between two people discussing the problem or the product.
**Best for:** Younger demographics, consumer apps, gifting, or impulse purchases.
**Example:**
Blue bubble: "What did you get Mom for her birthday?"
Gray bubble: "That custom star map thing. She cried."

## 10. The "Brand Manifesto"

**What it is:** An aspirational, high-energy declaration of what the brand stands for and who it is for. Usually features premium, high-production-value photography.
**Best for:** Luxury brands, fitness apparel, community-driven products.
**Example:** "For the ones who don't hit snooze." overlaid on a gritty, cinematic shot of a runner at 5 AM.

## 11. The "Lifestyle / Emotional Resonance"

**What it is:** A cinematic, scene-based lifestyle image paired with an emotional headline that captures a specific psychological state rather than a functional benefit.
**Best for:** High-ticket coaching, personal development, luxury travel, or products addressing deep emotional pain points.
**Example:** A moody shot of a founder sitting in their car in the driveway at dusk. Headline: "You won the day. So why are you dreading walking through your own front door?"

## 12. The "Framework Explanation"

**What it is:** A consulting-style visual framework (pyramids, flywheels, Venn diagrams) explaining a mechanism with structured shapes and labels.
**Best for:** B2B services, coaching programs, complex software, or paradigm-shifting methodologies.
**Example:** Two pyramids side-by-side. The left shows the flawed "Standard Approach" (Mindset at the top, Nervous System at the bottom). The right shows the "New Method" inverted.

## 13. The "Authority Proof"

**What it is:** A striking founder or authority portrait combined with highlighted credentials and numeric proof of their expertise.
**Best for:** Expert-led businesses, agencies, consulting, or medical/health products.
**Example:** A black-and-white portrait of a founder. Text block: "Built and sold 4 companies. Trained 10,000 founders. Traveled to 85 countries."

## 14. The "Review / Rating Screenshot"

**What it is:** A screenshot (or stylized replica) of a real review from a platform like Amazon, Trustpilot, Yelp, or Google. The platform's native UI — stars, verified badge, reviewer name, date — provides the credibility.
**Best for:** Solution-aware audiences who trust third-party platforms more than brand-owned testimonials. Products with strong review volume.
**Example:** An Amazon review blown up to ad size. Five golden stars, "Verified Purchase" badge, title: "Genuinely changed my skin in 2 weeks." Review body underneath in readable type.

## 15. The "Tweet / Social Post Screenshot"

**What it is:** A screenshot of an unsolicited social post from a real customer — a tweet, Instagram caption, or TikTok comment — praising the product. Unretouched native UI.
**Best for:** Brands with organic social traction. Skeptical audiences who distrust paid endorsements.
**Example:** A tweet screenshot with the user's handle and profile picture visible: "Just canceled three subscriptions after getting [product]. Should've done this a year ago."

## 16. The "DM / Inbox Screenshot"

**What it is:** A stylized screenshot of the brand's own inbox — DMs, emails, or texts — showing messages from multiple happy customers aggregated in a single frame.
**Best for:** Brands early in their reputation-building phase with strong repeat customer love. Founder-led DTC.
**Example:** An iPhone Messages app screenshot showing preview lines from six different customer names: "Can I buy another one for my sister?" / "Day 30 update: I'm a believer." / "This is the third one I've bought this year."

## 17. The "Flat Lay / Product Stack"

**What it is:** An overhead shot of the product alongside its contents, accessories, or everything included in the box — styled on a clean surface. Communicates value through visible inventory.
**Best for:** Subscription boxes, supplement bundles, beauty kits, gifting products. Most-aware audiences evaluating what they get for the price.
**Example:** An overhead shot of a skincare starter kit: cleanser, serum, moisturizer, microfiber cloth, and a welcome card arranged on marble. Each item labeled with its name and purpose.

## 18. The "Product Hero / Studio Shot"

**What it is:** A single, cinematic product image on a clean or cinematic backdrop with minimal or no copy. The product's design does the persuasion.
**Best for:** Premium brands where the product itself is the argument. Apparel, tech, home goods. Most-aware audiences.
**Example:** A single pair of selvedge denim jeans against a deep shadow backdrop, shot from a slightly heroic low angle. One line of copy in the corner: "Built to outlast you."

## 19. The "In-Use Action Shot"

**What it is:** A polished, intentional photograph of the product being actively used in its intended environment — branded lifestyle imagery, not UGC.
**Best for:** Products whose value is best shown in motion. Fitness gear, kitchen tools, outdoor equipment, beauty application.
**Example:** A cast-iron skillet mid-sear with steam rising, a chef's hand visible holding the handle, golden light from an open flame underneath.

## 20. The "Chart / Graph"

**What it is:** A real or stylized data chart — line graph, bar chart, dashboard screenshot — showing measurable progress or outcomes over time.
**Best for:** Fitness trackers, financial products, SaaS with measurable KPIs, performance supplements, any brand selling improvement.
**Example:** A line chart trending upward labeled "Weekly revenue — before and after." Headline: "What the first 90 days look like."

## 21. The "Review Count Hero"

**What it is:** Aggregate social proof as the dominant visual — star rating, total number of reviews, certification badges — rendered at massive scale. No single voice; the number is the argument.
**Best for:** Brands with serious review volume. Most-aware audiences in the final-decision stage.
**Example:** "4.8 ★ · 1,777 reviews · Vet Recommended" set in huge type on a warm background, with a small product shot off to one side.

## 22. The "As Seen In / Media Wall"

**What it is:** A horizontal strip of publication, podcast, or show logos that have featured the brand. Authority by association.
**Best for:** Brands with legitimate press placements. Higher-ticket purchases where third-party validation reduces risk.
**Example:** Logos for Forbes, Men's Health, The Wall Street Journal, and Bon Appétit in grayscale across the top third. Below: product hero and headline.

## 23. The "Problem → Agitate → Solution" Panel

**What it is:** A three-panel ad that walks a viewer through the classic direct response sequence in one image. Panel 1 names the problem, Panel 2 agitates it, Panel 3 reveals the product.
**Best for:** Problem-aware audiences who need to feel the pain before buying. Direct response categories — weight loss, pest control, sleep, productivity.
**Example:** Panel 1: a tangled knot of cables behind a desk. Panel 2: the same desk with red circles highlighting dust buildup. Panel 3: a clean cable management box with all wires tamed.

## 24. The "Before → During → After"

**What it is:** A three-panel progression showing state change over time — with a middle frame that makes the transition or mechanism visible, unlike the two-state Before/After Split.
**Best for:** Products whose value lies in the process, not just the outcome. Skincare protocols, fitness programs, home renovation.
**Example:** Panel 1: a cluttered pantry. Panel 2: hands labeling jars and sorting by category. Panel 3: the finished organized pantry with clear labels and visible inventory.

## 25. The "3-Panel Storyboard"

**What it is:** A narrative sequence across three panels that tells a micro-story — a character encountering a problem, using the product, and experiencing the outcome. More cinematic than Before → During → After.
**Best for:** Brands with strong narrative positioning. Founder-led products, lifestyle brands, cause-driven companies.
**Example:** Panel 1: a tired dad lacing up running shoes at 5 AM. Panel 2: him mid-stride on a quiet road, breath visible. Panel 3: him back home making pancakes with his kids, visibly energized.

## 26. The "Editorial / Magazine Style"

**What it is:** An ad styled to look like a page from a premium print magazine — serif typography, generous margins, editorial photography, pull quotes. Premium by aesthetic.
**Best for:** Luxury brands, fashion, fine dining, high-end wellness. Audiences who associate magazine design with quality.
**Example:** A black-and-white portrait of the founder holding the product, with a large serif pull quote: "We use more leather in one wallet than most brands use in five."

## 27. The "Press Mockup"

**What it is:** A stylized replica of a news article or feature about the brand — looks like it was clipped from a real publication.
**Best for:** Brands with real press coverage worth dramatizing, or brands establishing authority through implied media attention.
**Example:** A page laid out like The New York Times — serif headline, byline, two-column body: "The DTC brand quietly rewriting how America buys mattresses."

## 28. The "Founder Quote / Contrarian Take"

**What it is:** A first-person statement attributed to the founder — different from Bold Claim (unattributed, rhetorical) because the authority comes from who's saying it. Usually a contrarian or insider perspective.
**Best for:** Founder-led brands where the founder's voice is a brand asset. Agency services, coaching, category-disrupting products.
**Example:** "After 12 years in Big Skincare, I can tell you 80% of what's on the shelf is identical goo in different bottles. I started this brand to prove it." — [Founder name], CEO. Founder portrait alongside.
